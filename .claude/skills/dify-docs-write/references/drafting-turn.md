# The Drafting Turn

The standard is the style guide's "What a Good Page Does"; the rest of the guide says how to meet it, and the drafter reads all of it. This file is how a drafting turn holds itself to the standard.

## Open the turn with the reader

Fill this in from the task analysis and put it at the top of the turn that does the drafting, because a drafter who starts from the reader writes toward them:

> I'm writing [page or section] for [the persona, in one clause]. They arrive [what they were just doing] and need [what this text has to give them to keep moving]. With that in mind: [the drafting task].

## Per section, before drafting

One line: the reader's moment and the question they bring to this section. One sentence: the judgment the section owes them, the thing they could not get from the interface or from common sense. Draft from those two lines.

The research summary is the ceiling (in co-writing, the research record's verified facts are), not a checklist. No product fact enters from outside it, and not every fact on it does: a fact enters because that reader would ask for it here, and the rest stays on the summary. What the reader should do with a fact is the drafter's to say. What the summary lists as visible in the UI, or as another page's, stays out even when it would be easy to say. When a sentence depends on what another page carries, open that page; the pages this one links to and its siblings are yours to read, and the reference page still sets the register.

## Read it back

Before any mechanical check, read the whole draft once as its reader, first time on the page, against the whole style guide: its opening section, "What a Good Page Does", is the standard, and its later sections are the rules a page also has to meet. Read the guide just before the read-back, unless you read it to draft this page in the same turn.

Stop at every sentence that stumbles when read aloud, every noun phrase doing a verb's work, every sentence carrying two separable ideas, every fact the reader would not have asked for, every slot kept for a template's sake. Stop as well at a clause hung on a finished sentence behind a semicolon or a colon, a pronoun whose referent is not the nearest noun, and a word or connective the next sentence repeats. Fix those first. The linters measure what is left.

The read-back that counts is of the text you are about to show, after its last edit. Revising moves sentences, so read the revised section again as its reader, then its structure: whether it still opens with the action, keeps the reader's order with each fact at the step where they act on it, and takes the form, prose, list, or steps, that fits what they do there. A rewrite you propose in a co-writing walk is held to this as much as a draft is. Reading the original to find its faults does not read the rewrite.
